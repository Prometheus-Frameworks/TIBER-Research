## Purpose

Use one bounded real-world event to test whether TIBER can translate the loss of a non-fantasy "infrastructure" player through the offense without applying a narrative-only blanket downgrade.

Core question:

> When an elite starting tackle becomes unavailable, how are protection burden, play design, quarterback conditions, receiving opportunity, backfield participation, and fantasy-point opportunity redistributed?

This is an extension of Opportunity Redistribution. The missing object is not a fantasy player's targets or carries. It is a protection constraint that can be redistributed to:

- the replacement tackle;
- tight ends and running backs through chips, double teams, or full protection assignments;
- the quarterback through faster decisions, movement, scrambles, throwaways, and added exposure;
- the play caller through formations, personnel, protection count, under-center play action, quick game, rollouts, and run direction; and
- receivers through changes in route participation, target timing, depth, air yards, and explosive-play probability.

A player can gain snaps because he is protecting while gaining no routes or fantasy opportunity. That distinction must remain first-class.

## Atomic claims

Assess each claim independently as:

`supported | contradicted | mixed | insufficient | out_of_scope`

1. **Availability and replacement state**  
   Tunsil is unavailable for the relevant observation window, Coleman is the actual replacement, and the surrounding line configuration is correctly identified.

2. **Protection-quality pressure**  
   Replacing Tunsil with Coleman creates a material expected reduction in isolated protection quality or increases the need for schematic help.

3. **Quarterback and play-calling response**  
   The absence changes protection count, pressure conditions, time to throw, scramble/throwaway behavior, play action, quick game, rollout usage, depth of target, or explosive-pass opportunity.

4. **Backfield constraint redistribution**  
   Washington increases running-back protection or chip participation, and any additional Rachaad White snaps are correctly separated into pass blocks, chip-and-release routes, full routes, carries, and targets. Determine whether Jacory Croskey-Merritt loses passing-down participation without assuming an early-down carry loss.

5. **McLaurin opportunity versus efficiency**  
   Determine separately whether McLaurin's target/first-read opportunity changes and whether his air yards, target depth, play-action/deep usage, YPRR, explosive conversion, red-zone opportunity, or touchdown ceiling changes.

Do not collapse opportunity and efficiency into one verdict.

## Preregistered response branches

### H1 — Replacement holds

Coleman remains serviceable without materially greater help.

Expected signals:

- stable chip/double-team and six-/seven-man protection rates;
- stable quick-pressure and sack rates;
- stable play-action/deep-attempt profile;
- stable McLaurin air-yard opportunity; and
- no meaningful White/JCM passing-down redistribution attributable to protection.

### H2 — Successful adaptation

Washington preserves quarterback conditions by redistributing protection and changing play design.

Expected signals:

- more chips, double teams, tight-end or running-back protection;
- more quick game, under-center play action, movement, or rollouts;
- McLaurin target opportunity holding better than target depth or explosive efficiency;
- White gaining passing-down snaps, with routes and pass blocks reported separately; and
- JCM early-down rushing remaining more stable than his third-down/two-minute role.

### H3 — Protection failure

Adaptation does not fully compensate for the replacement.

Expected signals:

- higher quick-pressure, pressure-to-sack, hit, throwaway, or scramble rates;
- lower depth of target and explosive-pass frequency;
- fewer sustained and scoring drives;
- lower passing efficiency for Daniels; and
- offense-wide efficiency pressure that cannot be represented as one player's lost workload.

The research may support different branches in different game contexts or against different edge-rusher quality. Do not force one season-long branch prematurely.

## Required observation fields

### Protection

- five-, six-, and seven-man protection rate;
- individual pass-block and pressure attribution;
- double-team and chip rate;
- running-back and tight-end pass-block snaps;
- quick pressure under 2.5 seconds;
- overall pressure and sack rate;
- time to first pressure;
- pressure-to-sack conversion; and
- opponent/pass-rusher context.

### Scheme and quarterback response

- under-center and shotgun rate;
- play-action rate;
- time to throw;
- designed movement and rollout rate;
- scramble and throwaway rate;
- average depth of target;
- deep-attempt and explosive-pass rate; and
- EPA/success rate per dropback, with context.

### Fantasy participation

- routes per team dropback;
- pass-block, chip-and-release, and full-route participation;
- third-down and two-minute snaps;
- early-down and red-zone carry share;
- target, first-read, and air-yard shares;
- target timing before/after 2.5 seconds; and
- player-level fantasy output reported as an outcome, not proof of mechanism.

## Counterfactual guardrails

Do **not** use a naive 2025 Tunsil on/off team split as causal evidence. Coleman's late-season LT starts coincided with changes in quarterback availability, opponents, game state, and surrounding personnel.

Coleman's two pressures on 87 late-2025 LT pass-blocking snaps may establish a credible non-catastrophic branch. It must not be treated as a sufficient offense forecast.

A later quantitative design may use a matched historical event study of starting-tackle losses, but only if it can govern:

- exact injury/availability timing;
- starter and replacement quality;
- quarterback identity and health;
- opponent pass-rush quality;
- game state and play-calling;
- surrounding line injuries;
- source revisions and missingness; and
- pre-event trends.

If those controls are unavailable, report the counterfactual as unresolved.
